# 07 — Update Cadence

Chapter 04 ended with the model being frozen into a *release* — a
finished vintage you can chat with. This chapter answers the natural
follow-up:

> **How often does the shelf change, what changes quietly behind your
> back, and how do you keep up without chasing?**

## The big releases: re-cooking the whole meal

A new numbered version of a model is a whole restaurant re-opened:

- Training re-run — longer, on a fresh and larger library (chapter 02).
- Finishing school refreshed with new examples (chapter 04).
- The tasting panel re-assembled (chapter 04 again).
- Months of work, a full vintage, a name that's slightly different.

That's why major releases arrive with headlines and reading guides:
they genuinely are different *people* wearing similar names. Expect
anywhere from a few months to a year between the flagships, families
moving on their own seasons.

## The quiet updates: re-seasoning the kitchen

Meanwhile, the chat service you use changes more often than the model
inside it. Around the frozen model sits a working kitchen:

- **The waitstaff instructions** — the hidden system prompt that sets
  tone and rules for every conversation. Adjusting its wording can
  noticeably change replies. This can change any week, without
  announcement.
- **The reading tools** — many chat systems are allowed to *fetch
  fresh pages* (a real web search, your uploaded documents) before
  answering. If that forager gets added or retuned, "the same model"
  suddenly answers differently about anything recent.
- **The guardrail seasoning** — small daily-scale adjustments to what
  gets refused and how.
- **The serving temperature** — default settings can shift subtly;
  if your app exposes the dial (chapter 03), you control it; if not,
  the kitchen does.

So when your chats feel different but the model name hasn't changed,
the kitchen is the likely culprit: the vintage is frozen; the
seasoning program isn't.

## What to do about it — the gentle protocol

A friendly habit beats a news-chasing habit:

1. **Note your model's version vintage** (chapter 06) in the first
   line of a working session's notes: "Toy-v3-Talk, March-vintage."
   Then, when behavior confuses you, you know where to look first —
   did the model change, or only the kitchen?
2. **Skim release notes, don't hunt rumors.** The family's own
   release page, once per cycle, beats a week of hot takes.
3. **Ask the model its cutoff — then verify with a doc.** "What's the
   latest date you were trained on?" is a fair question; the answer
   is usually right and occasionally recalled fuzzy, so don't pin
   anything important to it without a second source.
4. **Re-run your little trial drills after a big jump.** The three
   questions you care about — the same way you'd test-drive after a
   suspension change.
5. **Don't chase every minor increment.** Beyond your own drill
   scores and honest notes, sub-version differences are usually
   background noise for everyday work.

## Your saved prompts outlive every model

Your prompt library, notes, and formats are the *most* durable part
of your AI setup. When you find a prompt that works beautifully,
label it with what it was tuned for and the vintage it worked on
(e.g., "handout tone DIY — Toy-v3-Talk, March"), because prompts are
dishes and vintages are cooks: a dish usually survives the change of
cook with minor adjustment, not reinvention.

A quick note for the local-model readers following along with
`local-ai-handbook`: your downloaded model never re-seasons itself.
Version-vintage changes arrive as a distinct download and a
re-install — which is exactly why local-AI people tend to know their
vintage precisely.

## The guide's close of the journey

That's the full story — reading, guess-the-next-word, finishing
school, tasting panel, the inventions that occasionally slip through,
the four-part names, and now the seasons. One word of honest
encouragement to close: none of this makes the models less
impressive. It makes them *usefully* impressive — the difference
between being dazzled by a cook and being a regular at their table.
